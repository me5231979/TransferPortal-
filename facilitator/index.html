01 · A week before

  2. Confirm the current state of the actual Transfer Portal rollout with People, Culture and Belonging: what is live, what is coming, and who staff contact for help. Write those three facts where you can reach them mid-session.

05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Teach from the printed quick reference: the guarantees, the six entrances, the two tracks, and the manager script all work on a whiteboard. The pair activities never needed a screen; the capstone moves to the printed card.
If Learner devices can't join (wifi or filters): Run facilitator-only: the trainers play well on the projector with the room voting A/B/C by hands, and the capstone moves to paper. You lose typing, not learning.
If You're 10+ minutes behind by the culture section (04): Declare the Core path silently: trainers stay, pair debriefs shrink to one voice, glossary is pointed at. Never cut Section 04's trainer, the recap, or the capstone; they carry the program's success.
If Someone shares a live, painful situation, a role elimination or a conflict with their manager: Honor it and bound it: 'That's real, and you deserve a better answer than a training room can give. Entrance E and the employee-consultant conversation exist for exactly this; let's connect after.' Follow up privately with the People, Culture and Belonging contact you prepped. Keep names off the whiteboard.
If A manager pushes back hard: 'so HR built a tool to raid my team': Don't argue; go to evidence. Ask them what it costs to backfill an external departure, then show the retention numbers and the hoarding research: the choice is between losing people to another Vanderbilt unit or to another employer. Then land the backfill cascade: their next great hire comes through the same portal.
If The room distrusts the privacy claims ('HR always says that'): Slow down and be concrete: interest declarations trigger no notifications, data reaches leaders only aggregated through Workforce Intelligence, and asking HR to work around that is itself a violation managers are trained on. Then write the residual doubts on the open-questions board and commit to written answers; skeptics convert on follow-through, not reassurance.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

So my manager can see everything I do in there?

No. Your browsing, your Careers of Interest, and your skills profile are private to you. Your manager gets no notification when you declare interest, and leader-facing Workforce Intelligence shows aggregated, privacy-preserving data, never a list of who is looking. Your manager learns you applied for a role when you tell them or when the hiring process formally requires it, and the norm is that you have that conversation yourself once you are a serious candidate.

Isn't this just poaching with extra steps?

Poaching is secret and zero-sum. This is the opposite on both counts: the marketplace is open to every unit, the releasing manager gets a planned handoff and a backfill through the same system, and the alternative to an internal move is usually an external resignation, which has no handoff and no backfill window. The research backs it: about 75% of managers admit to talent hoarding, and hoarding deters internal applications without stopping external ones (Haegele, American Economic Review, 2024).

If I take a Gig and do great work, why doesn't that guarantee me the permanent role?

Because the Gig is development and the requisition is a competition, and keeping them separate protects you both ways. If Gigs converted automatically, every Gig would become a quiet audition and managers would guard them like headcount. Kept separate, you can stretch without betting your reputation, and when the real requisition opens, your Gig gives you evidence, relationships, and a warm start most candidates won't have.

What if my manager retaliates anyway?

Then the program's integrity rules are on your side: blocking an application, downgrading a rating in response, or excluding someone from key work are named as serious violations, and managers are trained on that before the portal reaches them. Document what happened and bring it to your HR business partner or an employee consultant. And a candid note: the guardrails only bite because leadership enforces them, which is exactly why manager training like this session exists.

Why would I tell the truth about my skill levels? Inflating them gets me matched to better roles.

It gets you matched to interviews you then lose. The hiring manager verifies skills in the interview, and an inflated profile spends your credibility exactly where you need it most. Honest levels do something better: they route you to roles and Gigs where you'll genuinely compete, and they turn the gaps into Grow goals instead of surprises.

We're short-staffed already. How is my unit supposed to survive its best people leaving?

Two honest answers. First, the counterfactual: your best people already have a marketplace; it's called every other employer, and it offers no handoff, no knowledge transfer, and no internal-first backfill. The portal keeps that talent at Vanderbilt and gives you two to four weeks of coordinated transition plus first look at internal candidates for the backfill. Second, the pattern compounds: units known for developing and releasing people attract the strongest internal applicants, so the pipeline runs both directions.

Section 01 · What It Is

Your career, in one place.

The Transfer Portal brings internal career movement into a single Oracle-powered experience: open internal requisitions with a two-week internal-first window, Careers of Interest, your skills profile, Grow development plans, performance history, succession slates, and Gigs, which are short-term stretch assignments. Leaders see the same underlying data through Workforce Intelligence, aggregated and privacy-preserving, built for workforce planning rather than watching individuals.

You control

What you share

Your profile, your Careers of Interest, your Gig and requisition applications, and when you tell your manager. Declaring interest notifies no one.

HR guarantees

Privacy by default

No auto-notification to your manager, a two-week internal-first window on eligible requisitions, and advisory support that activates only when you ask.

Managers commit

A fair marketplace

No retaliation, no surveillance, coaching conversations, respectful handoffs, and partnership on the backfill.

In practice

The clean way to hold it: the Transfer Portal is a competitive internal marketplace, and the word that matters is yours. It is a supported path with clear norms rather than a way around your manager. Gigs are optional stretch work, and permanent moves run through real requisitions. Interest declarations roll up to People, Culture and Belonging only in aggregate. And nothing HR-initiated happens to you through it: advisory support activates when you ask, never before.

Section 02 · Entrances

Six entrances, one portal.

People arrive at a career question from different places, so the portal has more than one door. Find the one that sounds like you today. You can change lanes any time.

A · The Explorer

"I'm curious, but not ready to move"

Generally happy, wondering what else exists here.

Enter through: Careers of Interest and your skills profile. You browse and declare privately; tailored role and learning suggestions start arriving. Your manager is not notified.

B · The Tenured High Performer

"I need a new challenge"

Deep institutional knowledge, plateaued in the current role.

Enter through: Gigs plus Careers of Interest. A short-term stretch assignment in another unit, while keeping your home role. Gigs count as development and never promise a permanent move.

C · The Underutilized Contributor

"I have skills I never get to use"

Hired for one thing, grew into others, no outlet.

Enter through: a skills profile update plus requisition search. Tag skills at their true level and the portal surfaces roles and Gigs that need them.

D · The Post-Reorg Realigner

"My role feels misaligned"

Team, mission, or manager just changed materially.

Enter through: an employee-consultant conversation plus requisitions. An advisor helps you clarify what you want next; you apply competitively. Advisory starts when you ask, never before.

E · The At-Risk Employee

"I've heard my role may be at risk"

Restructure, elimination, or budget pressure looming.

Enter through: advisory plus priority requisition matching. Proactive support to find roles where your skills transfer, with expedited internal review.

F · The Direct Applicant

"There's an open job I want"

Knows the role, knows they're ready.

Enter through: the internal requisition listing. Apply inside the two-week internal window, interview competitively, and if selected, transition with a coordinated handoff.

In practice

The entrances describe situations, never categories of people, and nobody is assigned one. Most staff will use two or three over a career: Explorer this year, Direct Applicant in three. The two advisory entrances (D and E) deserve a special word: the employee-consultant conversation is confidential, initiated by you, and nothing you share starts any action without your consent. For at-risk situations, entrance E adds priority matching and expedited review, which is the program working hardest exactly when someone needs it most.

Section 03 · The Process

Two tracks, both yours.

Everything in the portal runs on one of two tracks. Interest & Development is always on, private, and unhurried. Move starts when a requisition opens, and it is competitive. They live in the same place and feed each other.

Track 1 · Interest & Development, always on
  1. Build your profile. Skills tagged at honest levels, Careers of Interest marked. Private to you; rolls up only in aggregate.
  2. Set a Grow plan. One to three development goals tied to a role, skill, or stretch you want.
  3. Explore. Browse requisitions, pathways, and Gig postings. Save what interests you.
  4. Take a Gig, optionally. Defined scope, defined duration, real development. Never a trial for a transfer, never a guarantee.
  5. Ask for advisory support, optionally. An employee-consultant conversation, on your initiative. Nothing you share triggers action without your consent.
Track 2 · Move, event-driven
  1. A requisition opens and posts internally for two weeks before going external.
  2. You apply. The hiring manager sees what you choose to share. Performance data follows Vanderbilt policy; it is never surfaced casually.
  3. You interview, in the same process internal and external candidates get.
  4. Transition is coordinated. HR and both managers plan the handoff, typically two to four weeks. The releasing manager is a partner.
  5. You onboard fast, because your institutional knowledge comes with you.
  6. Your old role backfills through the same portal, so the releasing team gets the same first look you just used.

Play · Call the next move

Why this matters: knowing what happens next, at each step, is what makes the portal feel safe enough to actually use. Five moments in a real move; call what the process does.

Go deeper

In practice

The disclosure question deserves a straight answer. You are never required to tell your manager the day you declare an interest, browse a role, or update your skills. You are expected to have a professional conversation once you are a serious candidate, before your manager learns about it from a requisition workflow. That timing is yours to choose, and choosing it well is part of competing respectfully. The other quiet rule: internal application never equals internal entitlement. The window gives you first look; the interview is still yours to win.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: the two-track model clicks when you trace one imaginary colleague all the way through it.

  1. Pairs: invent a colleague (role, not a real name) and pick their entrance from Section 02.
  2. Walk them through Track 1: what would they build, save, or ask for this month?
  3. Now a requisition opens that fits. Walk Track 2 to the handoff. Where would it wobble, and who fixes it?

Flying solo? Trace yourself instead: your Track 1 this month, and the Track 2 you'd want ready if the right posting appeared next quarter.

  4. Debrief the disclosure question from Go deeper: no announcement needed to explore; a professional conversation once you're a serious candidate.
  5. Run the pair trace: invent a colleague, walk both tracks, find where it would wobble.
Ask

“Where in these two tracks would a real move at Vanderbilt most likely wobble, and who's responsible for catching it?”

Expect:

  • The handoff: the old team's workload during transition
  • The disclosure conversation going badly
  • Managers ask who pays for overlap or backfill gaps

Respond: Sort the answers into the structure: the handoff has an owner (HR plus both managers, on a timeline), the disclosure conversation is the next section entirely, and the resourcing questions go on the open-questions board for People, Culture and Belonging with a promised written answer.

Debrief

Private until you act, competitive when you do, coordinated when you win. If you can say those three phrases, you can walk both tracks.

Validate: 4+ of 5 on the trainer; pairs can name where a move would wobble and who fixes it (usually the handoff, fixed by HR plus both managers).

Watch for: The 'guaranteed job' misread and its mirror, 'so applying is pointless.' The correction is the same sentence both times: the window guarantees you first look, and the interview is still yours to win. Also watch for Gig-equals-tryout creeping back; the trainer addresses it, but repeat it if you hear it in the pairs.

Transition

“Everything so far works on paper. Whether it works at Vanderbilt comes down to one conversation, and one word managers keep using about it.”

Section 04 · The Culture Shift

Let's talk about poaching.

Some managers will hear "internal mobility" and think "poaching." That reaction is predictable, and it has been measured: in the first large-scale study of talent hoarding, about three quarters of managers admitted to hoarding, it was strongest where managers are judged on team performance, and it measurably deters people from applying internally (Haegele, American Economic Review, 2024). If we do nothing, the average manager will quietly suppress the exact behavior this portal exists to enable. So the culture work is the work.

The reframe every manager needs

When a team member grows into a new role at Vanderbilt, that is a win for your leadership record. Every internal move is retention we would otherwise have lost to an outside offer, skills recombining where the university needs them, and a backfill that brings your own team fresh capability.

The response that builds the culture

"Thank you for telling me. Tell me what drew you to it. What can I do to help you compete well, and whatever the outcome, what should we plan for next in your development here?"

The response the culture cannot survive

"After everything I've invested in you?"

The first response builds a career at Vanderbilt. The second builds a resume for somewhere else.

Manager guardrails

No retaliation: blocking an application, downgrading a rating, or sidelining someone who applied is a serious violation. No surveillance: you are not notified of interest declarations; asking HR to work around that is off the table. Do coach: hold the "what's next for you here" conversation before a requisition holds it for you.

Play · Judge the response

Why this matters: the whole program lives or dies in the sixty seconds after a team member says "I've applied for something." Five manager responses; call each one.

Go deeper

In practice

Hoarding is rarely villainy; it is incentives. Haegele's research found it worst where managers are evaluated on team performance, teams are large, and talent is visible, which describes most of us on a normal Tuesday. So the rollout counters structure with structure, following Kotter's change model: urgency built from retention risk and vacancy costs, champion managers in every unit who volunteer to release talent and get celebrated for it, barriers removed through the internal-first window and structured handoffs, early anonymized "first mover" stories, and eventually a manager release rate on leader scorecards. Staff hold guardrails too: honest skill ratings, respectful competition, and a professional disclosure conversation once you're a serious candidate.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: drafting the words before the moment arrives is what makes the good response the one that actually comes out.

  1. Managers: draft the two sentences you'll say the next time a team member tells you they've applied. Staff: draft how you'd open the disclosure conversation with your manager.
  2. Pairs, one of each where possible: read them to each other. Does the manager line pass the "thank you first" test? Does the staff line land before the requisition would?
  3. Volunteers read the best lines to the room. Collect them; they become the unit's shared script.

Flying solo? Draft both lines anyway, whichever chair you sit in today. You will eventually sit in the other one.

Facilitator scriptFull 18 min · Core 12

The session's center of gravity: name talent hoarding as a measured, incentive-driven pattern, install the mobility-is-development reframe, and drill the manager response the whole program depends on.

Say

“Here's the sentence some managers are already thinking: 'this is poaching.' Let's take it seriously, because it isn't irrational. Researchers got personnel records from a major firm and measured what managers actually do: about three quarters admit to talent hoarding, holding onto their best people instead of releasing them to grow. It's worst exactly where good management is rewarded: team performance incentives, big teams, visible stars. And it works, in the worst way: hoarding measurably deters people from applying internally. It does nothing to stop them applying externally. So the real choice was never 'keep them or lose them.' It's 'lose them to the third floor, with a handoff and a backfill, or lose them to another employer with two weeks' notice.' When a team member grows into a new role at Vanderbilt, that is a win on your leadership record. And there's one moment where that either becomes true or dies: the moment they tell you they've applied.”

Do
  1. Land the research first and slowly: about 75% of managers self-report hoarding, it's worst under team-performance incentives with large teams and visible talent, and it deters internal applications (Haegele, AER 2024).
  2. Say the implication plainly: unmanaged, the average manager suppresses exactly the behavior the portal needs. Nobody has to be a villain for the program to fail.
  3. Walk the reframe card: an internal move is retention we'd have lost, skills recombining, and a backfill opportunity, all three at once.
  4. Read both script cards aloud, the culture-building response and the killer, and let the second one sit in silence for a beat.
  5. Walk the guardrails: no retaliation, no surveillance, do coach. Name them as integrity rules with teeth, not suggestions.
  6. Send them to Judge the Response: five manager replies, call each one.
  7. Run the drafting activity: managers draft their two sentences, staff draft their disclosure opener, pairs exchange, volunteers read to the room.
  8. In the debrief, mention the structural counterweights from Go deeper: Kotter-style rollout, champion managers, first mover stories, release rate on scorecards.
Ask

“Managers, honestly: what makes the generous response hard in the actual moment, even when you believe in it?”

Expect:

  • The immediate coverage problem: who does the work
  • It genuinely feels personal after years of investment
  • Fear of a cascade: one leaves and three follow

Respond: Honor all three, then sort them: coverage has structure behind it (the handoff timeline and internal-first backfill). The personal sting is real, and it's exactly what the reframe retrains. The cascade fear inverts under the research: units that develop and release people attract internal talent; units that trap them create the exodus they feared.

Debrief

Hoarding is incentives, so the fix is structure plus one practiced sentence: thank you for telling me. The guardrails hold the floor, the reframe raises the ceiling, and the backfill cascade pays the bill.

Validate: 4+ of 5 on the trainer; the room can distinguish hoarding (guilt, stalling) from guardrail violations (blocking, retaliation); every manager leaves with two drafted sentences.

Watch for: Two failure modes: manager shame (the research says incentives, so keep it structural and blame-free) and staff glee (the point is partnership, so pull it back to the backfill cascade: today's releasing manager is next month's hiring manager). If a real manager-staff pair sits together, don't force them to exchange drafts; let them choose.

Transition

“You now have the words. For the meeting where you'll need ammunition instead, here are the numbers.”

Section 05 · The Evidence

The case, in numbers.

This section is built to be borrowed: every claim is published, cited, and linked, ready for an executive briefing or a hallway debate. First, see how well your instincts match the research.

Play · Guess the number

Why this matters: a number you guessed at and missed sticks far better than one you were shown. Five findings; call each before the reveal.

Retention

70% vs 45%

Odds of staying for employees promoted within three years of hire, versus those who never moved. Lateral moves land at 62%. SHRM / LinkedIn, 32 million profiles.

Tenure

5.4 vs 2.9 years

Average tenure at high-mobility organizations versus low. Internal movers are 40% more likely to stay three years or more. Inop.ai benchmark synthesis; LinkedIn Global Talent Trends 2024.

Engagement

~60% higher

Engagement scores reported by employees after an internal move, plus a meta-analysis of 56 studies (N = 284,086) tying rotation to satisfaction, commitment, and lower burnout. Inop.ai; Mlekus & Maier.

Speed

20 to 25% faster

How much sooner internal hires reach full competency; internal-first hiring also cuts time-to-hire by roughly 10 to 20 days. LinkedIn Workplace Learning Report; Bersin Factbook.

Cost

Up to 3 to 5x

Full cost of hiring externally once time and lost productivity count; direct costs alone run 18 to 20% higher. Bersin; SHRM 2024 benchmarking.

Quality

Internal > external

Cornell ILR: internal hires outperform external hires and stay longer, and the best internal hires are the likeliest to remain. Rotation-heavy firms are also disproportionately the innovative ones. Cornell ILR; Eriksson & Ortega.

Quick check: These outcomes are real, and they still won't happen at Vanderbilt automatically. Which finding explains why?

Go deeper

In practice

The one-sentence pitch, ready for an executive: "The Transfer Portal is Vanderbilt's staff-facing internal talent marketplace, a private, Oracle-powered experience where employees see internal opportunities first, signal interest safely, and compete for stretch work or new roles, while leaders see aggregated readiness and risk through Workforce Intelligence." Two more numbers for the skeptic: 93% of employees stay longer at organizations that invest in their careers (LinkedIn Workplace Learning Report), and industry internal-hire rates have slumped to about 24% from a 2020 peak near 40% (AMS), which is the gap Vanderbilt gets to win in.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: you retain what you can say out loud, with a source attached, under mild social pressure.

  1. Pairs: one of you is a skeptical department head with sixty seconds. The other makes the case for the portal using at least two cited numbers.
  2. Swap roles with two different numbers.
  3. Room debrief: which single statistic moved your skeptic most? Hands for retention, cost, speed, or the hoarding warning.

Flying solo? Write your own sixty-second version with the two numbers you'd actually use, and keep it where you can find it before your next leadership meeting.
