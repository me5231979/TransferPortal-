Section 01 · What It Is

It is two things.

The Talent Transfer Portal is Vanderbilt's private home for growing your career inside the university. It lives in Oracle, and it splits into two paths.

1 · Career moves

New roles at Vanderbilt

Find open Vanderbilt jobs and compete for them. Staff see new postings first, for five business days. Flag the careers that interest you, and the portal matches roles to your skills. Private until you apply.

2 · Gigs

Short-term opportunities

Short projects in other units that build your skills. We are rolling Gigs out in Oracle to promote short-term opportunities. Your manager approves each Gig, you keep your home job, and a Gig never promises a transfer.

The promises

You control what you share. Showing interest alerts no one. Leaders see only group totals, never your activity. Managers may not punish or spy. Advisor help starts only when you ask.

Play · Fact or fiction

Why this matters: most fear comes from things the portal does not do. Five claims; true or false?

Go deeper

In practice

Two paths, one portal. Career moves are the long game: real jobs, won in fair interviews. Gigs are the short game: projects that stretch you now. Both run on the skills and interests you keep in Oracle, and both stay private until you act.

Section 02 · Entrances

Match your situation to its entrance.

Six doors, six common situations. Yours might be a mix. Every door starts best the same way: a growth conversation with your manager. That talk is recommended, never required, and exploring stays private until you say otherwise. Open a door to see the path.

A · The Explorer "I'm curious, but not ready to move"

You right now Happy enough, curious what else is out there, and worried that curiosity looks like quitting.

  1. Growth conversation with your manager recommended start
  2. Update the skills in your Talent Profile
  3. Flag Careers of Interest in the Grow area
  4. Browse the Opportunity Marketplace at your own pace
  5. Suggestions arrive as matching roles open

Where it leads A private map of your options that grows while you do your job.

Worth knowing Flagging interest alerts no one. You choose if and when to say more.

B · The Tenured High Performer "I need a new challenge"

You right now Years of know-how, great reviews, and work that now runs on autopilot.

  1. Growth conversation with your manager recommended start
  2. Browse Gigs in the Opportunity Marketplace rolling out
  3. Take one, with your manager's approval, scope and duration defined, home role intact
  4. Add the new skills to your Talent Profile
  5. Compete when the right requisition opens

Where it leads Stretch and fresh relationships now, and a stronger candidacy for the bigger next chapter.

Worth knowing Gigs are rolling out in Oracle to promote short-term opportunities. A Gig builds skills; it never promises a transfer. Real moves go through open job postings.

C · The Underutilized Contributor "I have skills I never get to use"

You right now Hired for one job, grew into more, and the extra skills sit unused.

  1. Growth conversation with your manager recommended start
  2. Tag every real skill at its true level in the Skills Center
  3. Point your Grow plan at the underused ones
  4. The portal surfaces roles and Gigs that need them
  5. Apply when the fit is real, or take a Gig

Where it leads Work that finally uses the whole toolkit, found by matching instead of luck.

Worth knowing Matching runs on your skills data. Honest levels beat long lists.

D · The Post-Reorg Realigner "My role feels misaligned"

You right now Your area was reorganized, and the job you signed up for changed under you.

  1. Growth conversation with your manager recommended start
  2. Request an employee-consultant conversation, confidential and yours to open
  3. Sort out the next chapter with your advisor
  4. Apply competitively to the requisitions that fit
  5. Transition through a coordinated handoff

Where it leads Clarity about the next chapter before you commit to chasing it.

Worth knowing You start the advisor talk, never HR, and nothing happens without your OK.

E · The At-Risk Employee "I've heard my role may be at risk"

You right now A restructure, a cut, or budget pressure may hit your role.

  1. Growth conversation with your manager recommended start
  2. Ask for advisory support and priority matching
  3. Your skills are matched proactively against open needs
  4. Faster review where your skills fit
  5. Land the next role before the risk lands

Where it leads The program working hardest exactly when you need it most.

Worth knowing Advisor talks stay private. Priority matching starts only when you ask.

F · The Direct Applicant "There's an open job I want"

You right now A posting is live, your skills match, and you are ready now.

  1. Growth conversation with your manager recommended start
  2. Apply inside the five-business-day internal-first window
  3. Interview in the same process every candidate gets
  4. HR and both managers plan the handoff, typically two to four weeks
  5. Onboard fast while your old role backfills through the portal

Where it leads A new chapter at Vanderbilt with your institutional knowledge riding along.

Worth knowing The five-day window gives you the first look. The interview is still yours to win.

Play · Pick the entrance

Why this matters: the doors only help if you can match them to real situations. Open a door or two above, then meet five colleagues and pick each one's door.

Go deeper

In practice

Doors are situations, never labels; most careers use two or three of them. The advisor doors (D and E) are private and yours to open, and E adds priority matching and faster review: the program working hardest when someone needs it most.

As a group · optional extension

Why this matters: claiming an entrance out loud turns an org chart into a personal plan, and hearing the room's spread proves every door gets used.

  1. Everyone privately picks the entrance closest to their situation today.
  2. Show of hands, A through F. Notice the spread; every room has all six.
  3. Pairs: tell your partner which you picked and what the first click would be.

Flying solo? Write down your entrance letter and the first thing you'd do inside it. Keep it; the capstone will ask for exactly this.

Section 03 · The Process

Grow every day. Compete when a job opens.

Growth here is a conversation, never a surprise. Keep your manager in the loop, and let the portal do the matching.

Every day · Build

Talk with your manager about your growth. Keep your Talent Profile and skills honest and current. Flag Careers of Interest in Grow, browse the Opportunity Marketplace, and take learning tied to your goals. Take a Gig when one fits; your manager approves it first. All of this stays private to you.

When a job opens · Compete

The job posts to Vanderbilt staff first, for five business days. You apply, sharing what you choose, and interview like any other candidate. If you win it, HR and both managers plan the handoff, usually two to four weeks. Your old job then posts the same way, so your team gets the same first look.

Play · Call the next move

Why this matters: the process feels safe when every next step is predictable. Five moments; call what happens.

Go deeper

In practice

The telling rule, put simply: you owe no one an announcement for exploring. Once you are a serious candidate for a role, tell your manager yourself, before the paperwork does. And applying from inside never means the job is yours; the window gives you the first look, and the interview is yours to win.

As a group · optional extension

Why this matters: the process clicks when you trace one imaginary colleague all the way through it.

  1. Pairs: invent a colleague (role, not a real name) and pick their entrance from Section 02.
  2. Walk their everyday building: what would they grow, save, or ask for this month?
  3. Now a requisition opens that fits. Walk them to the handoff. Where would it wobble, and who fixes it?

Flying solo? Trace yourself instead: what you'd build this month, and the application you'd want ready if the right posting appeared next quarter.

Section 04 · The Culture Shift

Releasing talent is a leadership win.

Some managers hear "internal mobility" and think "poaching." Research measured it: about three out of four managers admit to holding on to strong team members (talent hoarding), and that pressure stops people from applying inside (Haegele, American Economic Review, 2024). Left alone, hoarding starves the portal. The culture work is the work.

The reframe every manager needs

When a team member grows into a new role at Vanderbilt, that is a win on your record: we keep someone we would have lost, their skills land where the university needs them, and you get to bring fresh talent onto your team.

The response that builds the culture

"Thank you for telling me. Tell me what drew you to it. What can I do to help you compete well, and whatever the outcome, what should we plan for next in your development here?"

The response the culture cannot survive

"After everything I've invested in you?"

The first response builds a career at Vanderbilt. The second builds a resume for somewhere else.

Manager guardrails

No retaliation: blocking an application, lowering a rating, or sidelining an applicant is a serious violation. No watching: no alerts, no workarounds. Do coach: have the "what's next for you here" talk before a job posting has it for you.

The numbers, in case anyone asks

Staying

70% vs 45%

People promoted within three years stay at a 70% rate. People who never moved: 45%. SHRM / LinkedIn.

Years served

5.4 vs 2.9

Average years people stay where internal moves are common, versus where they are rare. Inop.ai.

Cost

3 to 5x

What an outside hire can cost compared to an inside move, once time and lost output count. Bersin.

Play · Judge the response

Why this matters: the program lives or dies in the minute after "I've applied for something." Five responses; call each one.

Go deeper

In practice

Hoarding comes from incentives, so the fix is structure: the staff-first posting window, planned handoffs, praise for managers who release talent, and a release rate on leader scorecards. Staff hold guardrails too: honest skill ratings, fair competition, and telling your manager at the right time.

As a group · optional extension

Why this matters: drafting the words before the moment arrives is what makes the good response the one that actually comes out.

  1. Managers: draft the two sentences you'll say the next time a team member tells you they've applied. Staff: draft how you'd open the disclosure conversation with your manager.
  2. Pairs, one of each where possible: read them to each other. Does the manager line pass the "thank you first" test? Does the staff line land before the requisition would?
  3. Volunteers read the best lines to the room. Collect them; they become the unit's shared script.

Flying solo? Draft both lines anyway, whichever chair you sit in today. You will eventually sit in the other one.
